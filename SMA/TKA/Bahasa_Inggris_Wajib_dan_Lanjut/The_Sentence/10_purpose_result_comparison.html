1.10 The Complex Sentence: Purpose, Result and Comparison

So that, in order to, so…that, such…that, as…as, comparative + than, dan comparative clauses.

1. Purpose

Purpose menjelaskan tujuan. To / in order to + base verb digunakan ketika subject tindakan purpose sama atau dapat dipahami dari main clause: We organised a run to raise money. So that + clause menyediakan subject dan finite verb sendiri: We placed signs so that visitors could find the start.

PURPOSE: to / in order to + V   |   so that + S + V

2. Result

So + adjective/adverb + that: The event was so popular that registration closed early. So many/few + plural noun dan so much/little + uncountable noun juga membentuk result. Such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that: It was such a successful event that...

3. Comparison

As + adjective/adverb + as menyatakan kesetaraan. Comparative adjective/adverb diikuti than: The morning session was cooler than the afternoon session. Comparative clause dapat memiliki verb sendiri: We raised more money than we had expected.

FungsiPolaContoh

4. Bacaan TKA

Planning a Charity Run

A group of students organised a charity run to raise money for a village reading centre. They needed English materials for international guests and sponsors, including invitations, safety instructions, and a short report after the event. Their teacher noticed that the project naturally required three kinds of logical relationship: purpose, result, and comparison. These relationships are often expressed through complex sentence structures. Purpose explains why an action is done. The students wrote, “We opened online registration so that participants could reserve a place in advance.” They compared “so that” with infinitive patterns such as “to raise money” and “in order to raise money.” A clause with “so that” contains its own subject and verb, which allows the subject to differ from the main clause: “The organisers placed signs along the route so that runners could find each checkpoint easily.” Negative purpose can use “so that … not” or “in order not to,” depending on the structure. Result explains what happens because of a situation. “The event was so popular that registration closed early” uses “so + adjective/adverb + that.” “There were so many runners that the committee added another water station” uses “so many + plural noun + that.” With nouns, “such … that” is common: “It was such a successful event that the school plans to repeat it.” Students learned not to confuse these result structures with simple “so” as a coordinating conjunction. Comparison allows writers to express similarity or difference. The teacher reviewed “as … as” for equality: “The five-kilometre route was as popular as the shorter route.” Comparative adjectives can be followed by “than”: “The morning session was cooler than the afternoon session.” The class also examined clauses such as “The event raised more money than the committee had expected,” where the second part contains a verb and functions as a comparative clause. The students revised their materials with these structures in mind. In the safety guide, purpose clauses explained why participants should arrive early and why volunteers were placed at intersections. In the final report, result clauses connected attendance figures to organisational decisions. Comparisons helped the committee evaluate the event against previous school activities. On the morning of the run, more than four hundred participants arrived. The turnout was so large that volunteers had to create an additional registration line. However, the planning had been detailed enough that the event still began on time. The committee had placed signs so that visitors could move from the parking area to the starting point without asking for directions. After the run, students compared their fundraising target with the actual amount and discovered that they had collected nearly twice as much as expected. In reflection, the teacher asked why these grammar structures were useful. The students answered that purpose, result, and comparison make relationships explicit. A reader can see intention, consequence, and degree rather than having to infer them from separate sentences. For event reports, scientific explanations, and argumentative essays, these relationships are essential. The charity run therefore became not only a community project but also a practical lesson in how complex sentences organise cause, goals, outcomes, and evaluation.
