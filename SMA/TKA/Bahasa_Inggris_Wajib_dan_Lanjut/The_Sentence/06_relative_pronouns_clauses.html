1.6 The Complex Sentence: Relative Pronouns and Clauses

Who, whom, which, that; subject/object relatives; omission; defining vs non-defining.

1. Penjelasan Lengkap

Relative clause mengikuti antecedent (noun) dan menambahkan informasi tentang noun tersebut. Relative pronoun berfungsi sekaligus sebagai penghubung dan sebagai unsur sintaktis di dalam relative clause.

The artisan [who makes these baskets] lives nearby.   |   The cloth [that the artisan produced] won an award.

Who umum untuk manusia; whom adalah bentuk object yang lebih formal; which untuk benda/hewan; that dapat digunakan untuk manusia atau benda dalam banyak defining clauses. Jika relative pronoun menjadi subject, pronoun itu tidak boleh dihilangkan. Jika menjadi object dalam defining clause, ia sering dapat dihilangkan: The cloth (that) the artisan produced...

Hindari double subject/object: The loom that it stands near the window... salah karena that sudah menjadi subject. Pada non-defining clause, comma digunakan dan that tidak lazim: Mr Seno, who has worked here for thirty years, ...

PeranContoh
Subject personThe woman who teaches us is an artisan.
Object personThe artisan whom we interviewed was patient.
ThingThe tool which/that cuts the fibre is sharp.
Omitted object relativeThe cloth we bought was handmade.

2. Jebakan

✗ The woman who she makes baskets lives here.

✓ The woman who makes baskets lives here.

✗ Mr Seno, that is a woodcarver, demonstrated the technique.

✓ Mr Seno, who is a woodcarver, demonstrated the technique.

3. Bacaan TKA

Writing for a Local History Museum

A local history museum invited students to write short English descriptions for an exhibition about traditional crafts. The students collected information about artisans, tools, and objects, but their first descriptions repeated the same nouns again and again. One paragraph read, “The woman makes bamboo baskets. The woman learned the skill from her grandmother. The woman teaches young people.” The museum guide suggested using relative clauses to combine information and create smoother descriptions. Relative clauses usually follow a noun and give information about that noun. The words “who,” “whom,” “whose,” “which,” and “that” are common relative pronouns. For people, “who” is common as a subject: “The woman who makes these baskets lives near the river.” “Whom” can function as an object in formal English, although “who” is frequent in everyday usage. For things, “which” is common, and “that” can refer to people or things in many defining relative clauses. The teacher explained that the relative pronoun also has a grammatical role inside the relative clause, so students should not add an unnecessary second subject or object. The class compared “The loom that stands near the window is over fifty years old” with the incorrect “The loom that it stands near the window is over fifty years old.” Because “that” already represents “the loom” as the subject of the relative clause, “it” is redundant. They also studied object relative clauses: “The cloth that the artisan produced won an award.” Here, “that” represents the object of “produced,” so the clause already contains its own subject, “the artisan.” In many defining clauses, an object relative pronoun can be omitted: “The cloth the artisan produced won an award.” Students then considered the difference between defining and non-defining information. A defining relative clause identifies exactly which person or thing is meant. “The students who interviewed the artisan wrote the display text” refers specifically to those students. A non-defining clause adds extra information about an already identified noun: “Mr. Seno, who has worked as a woodcarver for thirty years, demonstrated the technique.” Non-defining clauses are separated by commas and do not normally use “that.” As the descriptions improved, students began to see relative clauses as a tool for information management. Instead of producing many short sentences, they could attach relevant details directly to the nouns they described. However, they also learned not to overload one sentence with too many relative clauses. Museum visitors read while standing and moving, so clarity mattered more than grammatical complexity for its own sake. When the exhibition opened, the English descriptions were concise but informative. Visitors could read about the tools that artisans used, the villages where certain crafts developed, and the people who preserved local techniques. The students were proud that their grammar work supported a real public project. Relative clauses had helped them connect facts without losing the identity of the people and objects at the centre of the story.
