1.9 The Complex Sentence: Reason and Contrast

Because, since, as, although, though, even though, whereas, while, despite, in spite of.

1. Reason Clauses

Because memberikan alasan eksplisit: Students support the rule because it is practical. Since/as juga dapat menyatakan reason, sering sebagai background yang dianggap diketahui atau kurang diberi tekanan.

CLAUSE + because/since/as + CLAUSE   |   because of + NOUN PHRASE

Because diikuti finite clause; because of diikuti noun phrase: because the road was closed vs because of the closure.

2. Contrast / Concession

Although/though/even though memperkenalkan concession: informasi subordinate tetap benar, tetapi main clause bergerak ke arah yang tidak sepenuhnya diharapkan. Whereas/while membandingkan dua fakta yang kontras. Despite/in spite of adalah preposition, sehingga diikuti noun phrase atau gerund, bukan finite clause biasa.

StrukturContoh
although + clauseAlthough uniforms are consistent, they do not solve every problem.
whereas + clauseSome value uniformity, whereas others value expression.
despite + NP/-ingDespite the cost, the policy remained popular.

✗ Despite the policy is popular, some students object.

✓ Although the policy is popular, some students object.

✓ Despite the policy's popularity, some students object.

✗ Because of the uniforms are affordable...

✓ Because the uniforms are affordable... / Because of their affordability...

3. Bacaan TKA

Debating School Uniforms

The debate club chose a familiar topic for its monthly forum: whether school uniforms should remain compulsory. Students had strong opinions, but their early drafts often listed claims without explaining why they believed them or how opposing ideas were related. The English teacher suggested focusing on clauses of reason and contrast, because persuasive writing depends on making logical relationships explicit. Reason clauses commonly begin with “because,” “since,” or “as.” “Many students support uniforms because they reduce visible differences in clothing” gives a direct reason. “Since the rule already exists, any change would require consultation” presents background reasoning that may be treated as already known. “As” can also introduce a reason, although it may be less emphatic than “because.” The teacher warned students not to confuse a clause introduced by “because” with the prepositional expression “because of,” which is followed by a noun phrase rather than a full subject–verb clause. Contrast can be expressed in several ways. “Although,” “though,” and “even though” introduce subordinate clauses that contrast with the main clause: “Although uniforms create a shared appearance, they do not guarantee equal treatment.” “Whereas” and “while” can compare two contrasting facts: “Some students value uniformity, whereas others value personal expression.” The students also reviewed “despite” and “in spite of,” which are prepositions and therefore normally take noun phrases or gerund constructions rather than finite clauses. The club analysed how punctuation and clause order affect emphasis. “Because uniforms are affordable in bulk, some families prefer them” begins with the reason and places a comma before the main clause. “Some families prefer uniforms because they are affordable in bulk” presents the claim first and then the reason, usually without a comma. Similarly, “Although the policy is popular with some parents, several students want more flexibility” foregrounds the concession before presenting the main point. To prepare for the forum, each team had to write an argument containing at least two reason clauses and two contrast clauses. The teacher encouraged them to avoid weak logic. A grammatically correct “because” does not automatically make a reason convincing. Students needed evidence to support the relationship. One team cited the cost of buying different clothes for school, while another questioned whether uniform rules actually reduce social pressure. During the debate, the structures helped speakers respond respectfully. Instead of simply saying an opponent was wrong, a student could say, “Although that argument explains the benefits of consistency, it does not address students who need different clothing for health or religious reasons.” Another speaker said, “We support a flexible policy because clear limits can exist without requiring identical clothing.” The forum ended without complete agreement, but the language became more precise. Students learned that reason and contrast clauses are central to analytical writing because they show how one proposition relates to another. They also learned that grammar cannot replace evidence or reasoning. It can, however, make the structure of reasoning visible, allowing readers and listeners to evaluate an argument more fairly.
