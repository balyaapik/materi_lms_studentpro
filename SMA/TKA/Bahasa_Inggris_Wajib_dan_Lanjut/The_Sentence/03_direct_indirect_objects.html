1.3 The Simple Sentence: Direct and Indirect Objects

Double-object construction, recipient, beneficiary, pola to/for, pronoun, dan verb restrictions.

1. Konsep dan Pola

Verb seperti give, send, show, teach, tell, offer, lend dapat melibatkan dua peserta sesudah verb: indirect object (recipient) dan direct object (thing transferred). Pola umum: S + V + IO + DO, misalnya The committee gave the judges a schedule.

SUBJECT→VERB→INDIRECT OBJECT→DIRECT OBJECT

Alternatifnya adalah S + V + DO + to/for + recipient/beneficiary: The committee gave a schedule to the judges. To menandai recipient/destination, sedangkan for sering menandai beneficiary: She bought Raka a ticket = She bought a ticket for Raka.

Tidak semua verb mengizinkan double-object pattern. Explain me the rule dan describe us the project tidak standar; gunakan explain the rule to me dan describe the project to us. Pronoun juga memengaruhi naturalness: Give it to me biasanya lebih natural daripada Give me it.

StrukturContohCatatan
V + IO + DOShe sent me the file.Recipient langsung sesudah verb.
V + DO + to + NPShe sent the file to me.Menonjolkan benda/destination.
V + DO + for + NPShe printed a copy for me.Beneficiary.

2. Jebakan TKA

✗ The teacher explained us the procedure.

✓ The teacher explained the procedure to us.

✗ Please give to the judges your report.

✓ Please give the judges your report. / Please give your report to the judges.

Tip: identifikasi dulu “apa yang dipindahkan” (DO) dan “siapa penerima/manfaatnya” (IO atau prepositional phrase).

3. Bacaan TKA

Objects in the Science Fair

For the annual science fair, the student committee had to coordinate equipment, certificates, and information for more than forty project teams. Because several visiting students spoke English, the committee prepared bilingual messages. At first, many messages sounded awkward. One notice said, “Please give to the judges your report.” Another read, “The committee sent the schedule the participants.” The meaning was understandable, but the object patterns were inconsistent. Their English teacher explained that many verbs can involve two objects: a person who receives something and the thing that is transferred. The teacher introduced the terms indirect object and direct object. In “The committee gave the judges a schedule,” “the judges” is the indirect object because they are the recipients, while “a schedule” is the direct object because it is the thing given. English often allows another pattern with a prepositional phrase: “The committee gave a schedule to the judges.” Both structures can be correct, but the choice depends on the verb, the length of the objects, emphasis, and natural usage. With verbs such as “give,” “send,” “show,” “teach,” and “offer,” the double-object construction is common. With some other verbs, only the prepositional pattern is acceptable. The committee practised by rewriting real announcements. “We sent the mentors the room numbers” became a useful model. When the direct object was long, however, students preferred the prepositional pattern: “We sent the final list of laboratory safety requirements to the mentors.” The teacher explained that heavy or complex information often sounds clearer later in the sentence. The class also discussed the difference between “to” and “for.” “To” usually marks a recipient or destination, as in “She handed the form to Raka.” “For” often marks a beneficiary, as in “She printed a copy for Raka.” This difference helped students avoid treating every second object in the same way. A further challenge involved pronouns. “Give me it” may be possible in limited contexts, but “Give it to me” is usually more natural. The students noticed that short pronouns often influence word order because English tends to place familiar, light information before heavier information. They also learned that not every verb permits two objects. “Explain me the procedure” is incorrect in standard English; the natural form is “Explain the procedure to me.” Similarly, “describe us the project” should be “describe the project to us.” During the fair, the committee used the revised messages on signs, email updates, and stage announcements. The clearer object patterns reduced confusion because recipients and transferred items were immediately identifiable. When a judge needed a replacement score sheet, a volunteer said, “I’ll bring you a new one,” rather than producing a long, hesitant sentence. After the event, the teacher asked the students what they had learned. They agreed that direct and indirect objects were not just labels for a grammar test. These patterns helped them organise real communication: who gives, shows, teaches, sends, buys, or tells what to whom. The science fair succeeded partly because the language of coordination had become as carefully structured as the experiments on display.
