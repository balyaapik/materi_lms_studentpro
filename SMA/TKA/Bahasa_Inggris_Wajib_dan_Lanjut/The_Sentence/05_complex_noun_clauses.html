1.5 The Complex Sentence: Noun Clauses

That-clauses, WH-clauses, whether/if clauses sebagai subject, object, complement, dan object of preposition.

1. Konsep Inti

Noun clause adalah dependent clause yang mengisi slot yang biasa ditempati noun phrase. Karena itu noun clause dapat menjadi subject, direct object, subject complement, atau complement setelah preposition tertentu.

[ What the students need ] is reliable access.   |   Teachers know [ that access is unequal ].

That-clause: We know (that) the data are incomplete. Dalam object position, that sering boleh dihilangkan dalam penggunaan informal. Sebagai subject, that biasanya dipertahankan: That the results changed is significant.

Embedded WH-question: direct question Where can students download it? berubah menjadi They asked where students could download it. Setelah WH-word digunakan statement word order, bukan question inversion.

Whether/if: yes/no question dapat diubah menjadi noun clause: We asked whether/if the device worked. Whether lebih fleksibel pada konteks formal, sebelum or not, dan setelah preposition.

FungsiContoh
SubjectWhat students need is flexibility.
ObjectTeachers know that access is unequal.
ComplementThe problem is that some students share devices.
Embedded questionWe asked where the file was stored.

2. Jebakan

✗ Students asked where could they download the file.

✓ Students asked where they could download the file.

✗ I wonder that whether the result is reliable.

✓ I wonder whether the result is reliable.

3. Bacaan TKA

What Students Think about Digital Learning

The student council conducted a survey about digital learning. They wanted to know whether students preferred printed textbooks, tablets, or a combination of both. When the results arrived, the council discovered that reporting opinions required more than simple sentences. Students wrote comments such as “I think online notes are useful” and “What teachers upload matters to me.” Their English teacher used the survey to explain noun clauses—dependent clauses that function in positions where a noun or noun phrase could appear. A noun clause can serve as a subject, object, complement, or object of a preposition. In “What the students need is reliable access,” the clause “what the students need” functions as the subject. In “Teachers know that internet access is unequal,” the “that”-clause functions as the object of “know.” After linking verbs, noun clauses can also function as complements: “The problem is that some students share devices at home.” The teacher emphasised that noun clauses contain a subject and a verb, yet they are embedded inside a larger sentence rather than standing independently in that role. Question-word noun clauses created the most difficulty. In a direct question, students ask, “Where can we download the file?” But when the question becomes part of a statement, normal statement word order usually returns: “Students asked where they could download the file,” not “where could they download the file.” The same principle applies to “what,” “when,” “why,” “who,” “how,” and “whether.” Yes/no questions can become noun clauses with “whether” or “if”: “The council asked whether students needed more computer time.” The teacher noted that “whether” is preferred in several formal structures, especially before “or not” and after prepositions. The council then analysed responses from the survey. One student wrote, “What I like most is the ability to search quickly.” Another wrote, “I am not sure whether digital notes help me remember information.” The class noticed that “that” is sometimes omitted when a that-clause functions as an object in informal English: “I think digital notes are useful.” However, omission is not always possible, especially when the clause is the subject. “That some students prefer paper is clear” requires “that” in standard formal writing. For their final report, the council used noun clauses to present uncertainty and evidence carefully. They wrote that most students wanted flexible access, but they also explained why some students still preferred printed books. The report included what students considered helpful, whether they had stable internet connections, and how often they used school devices. Because the grammar allowed whole propositions to function as sentence elements, the writers could discuss ideas rather than only people and things. The principal praised the report for being balanced. More importantly, the students understood why noun clauses are common in academic and formal English. Research writing constantly reports what people believe, what data show, whether a claim is supported, and why a result may have occurred. Noun clauses provide the grammatical machinery for packaging those complex ideas into larger sentences. Once students recognised the clause as a unit with a noun-like function, long sentences became easier to analyse and construct.
