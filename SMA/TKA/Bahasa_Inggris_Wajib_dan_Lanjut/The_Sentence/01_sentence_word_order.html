1.1 Sentence Word Order

Urutan kata dalam kalimat Inggris: pola inti, posisi modifier, adverb, complement, pertanyaan, dan jebakan terjemahan literal.

1. Tujuan Pembelajaran

  • Mengenali pola dasar Subject–Verb–Object/Complement (S–V–O/C).
  • Menempatkan adjective, adverb of frequency, adverb of manner, place, dan time secara natural.
  • Membedakan pola pernyataan dengan pertanyaan/negasi yang memakai auxiliary.
  • Menganalisis kesalahan word order akibat terjemahan langsung dari bahasa Indonesia.

2. Konsep Inti

Bahasa Inggris sangat bergantung pada word order untuk menunjukkan fungsi unsur kalimat. Dalam kalimat deklaratif netral, pola yang paling umum adalah Subject + Verb + Object/Complement. Informasi tambahan seperti manner, place, dan time dapat dipindah untuk fokus tertentu, tetapi hubungan subjek dan predikat harus tetap mudah dikenali.

SUBJECT→VERB→OBJECT / COMPLEMENT→MANNER · PLACE · TIME

Adjective biasanya muncul sebelum noun (a difficult test) atau sesudah linking verb sebagai subject complement (The test is difficult). Frequency adverbs seperti usually, often, never umumnya berada sebelum main verb tetapi sesudah be: She often studies at night; She is often tired.

Pada yes/no question, auxiliary mendahului subject: Do the students understand the rule? Pada WH-question pola umumnya WH + auxiliary + subject + main verb: Why did the editor change the sentence? Jika kata tanya sendiri adalah subject, tidak diperlukan inversion: Who wrote the article?

3. Pola Penting

FungsiPolaContoh
PernyataanS + V + O/CThe team revised the article.
Adverb frequencyS + Adv + V / S + be + AdvRina often edits. / Rina is often careful.

4. Jebakan Umum

✗ She every day reads the school newsletter.

✓ She reads the school newsletter every day.

✗ The students discussed carefully yesterday the article.

✓ The students discussed the article carefully yesterday.

✗ Why the editor changed the headline?

✓ Why did the editor change the headline?

Strategi TKA: cari dulu subject dan finite verb. Setelah inti kalimat jelas, baru cek object/complement dan posisi modifier. Jangan menilai urutan kata hanya dari “terdengar familiar”.

5. Bacaan TKA

Editing the School Newsletter

At Merdeka Senior High School, the English club was asked to redesign the school newsletter so that international visitors could understand it easily. The club had plenty of ideas, but the first draft was surprisingly difficult to read. The problem was not vocabulary. Most of the words were familiar. Instead, many sentences placed information in unusual positions. Some headlines began with long details about time and place, while the subject appeared much later. Other sentences separated the verb from its object with several unnecessary phrases. Readers had to stop, reread, and guess which words belonged together. The club adviser, Ms. Lestari, decided to turn the editing session into a lesson about sentence word order. She reminded the students that English usually prefers a clear core pattern: subject first, then verb, followed by an object or complement when needed. Adverbs and other details can be added, but their position should support meaning rather than hide it. For example, “The journalism team published the interview yesterday” is easier to process than a version that scatters the same information across the sentence. She also showed them that questions, negatives, and sentences with adverbs may change the surface order, but the relationship among subject, auxiliary, main verb, and object still needs to remain clear. To practise, the students examined one paragraph about the school’s recycling project. Rina highlighted subjects in blue, verbs in red, and objects in green. Dito underlined adverbs of time and place. When they compared their markings, they noticed that many confusing sentences had a simple solution: move the main subject and verb closer together. They also learned that English adjectives normally come before nouns, while many Indonesian modifiers can appear after nouns. This difference explained why literal translation sometimes produced unnatural English. Another issue involved frequency adverbs such as “usually” and “often”, which normally appear before the main verb but after the verb “be”. The team then created a checklist for every article. First, identify the subject and the main verb. Second, check whether a transitive verb has an object. Third, place complements and modifiers where readers can connect them easily to the words they describe. Fourth, examine special structures such as questions and sentences beginning with negative adverbials, because these may require inversion. Finally, read the sentence aloud. If the listener has to wait too long to discover who did what, the sentence probably needs revision. By the end of the afternoon, the newsletter looked very different. The students did not make every sentence short; instead, they made each sentence structurally transparent. Some sentences still contained long introductory phrases, but the main clause remained easy to locate. Others used adverbs at the beginning for emphasis, followed by a comma and a normal subject–verb pattern. The club learned that word order is not merely a mechanical rule. It guides readers through information, signals relationships among ideas, and helps writers control emphasis. When the revised newsletter was published, teachers commented that the English version felt more natural and professional. The students understood why: good word order allowed the meaning to arrive in the reader’s mind in the intended sequence.
