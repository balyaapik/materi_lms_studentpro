1.2 The Simple Sentence: Verbs With and Without Objects

Transitive, intransitive, linking verbs, object, complement, dan pola valensi verba.

1. Tujuan Pembelajaran

  • Membedakan transitive, intransitive, dan linking verbs.
  • Menentukan apakah sebuah verb membutuhkan direct object.
  • Membedakan direct object dari subject complement dan adverbial.
  • Mengenali verb yang dapat berubah pola sesuai konteks.

2. Penjelasan Lengkap

Transitive verb mengalihkan tindakan kepada object: The students borrowed three books. Pertanyaan borrowed what? dijawab oleh three books. Sebaliknya, intransitive verb tidak mengambil direct object: The children laughed loudly. Kata loudly adalah adverb, bukan object.

S + Vintransitive|S + Vtransitive + O|S + Linking V + Subject Complement

Linking verbs seperti be, become, seem, appear, feel, look dalam fungsi tertentu menghubungkan subject dengan complement yang menjelaskan atau mengidentifikasi subject: The room became quiet. Quiet bukan object karena tidak menerima tindakan.

Sebagian verb dapat dipakai transitively maupun intransitively. Bandingkan The meeting started at nine dengan The librarian started the meeting at nine. Karena pola verba tidak selalu dapat ditebak dari arti, penggunaan kamus learner sangat penting.

3. Pola dan Diagnosis

JenisPolaContoh
IntransitiveS + V (+ adverbial)The guests arrived early.
TransitiveS + V + OThe class discussed the novel.
LinkingS + V + CThe soup smells delicious.
AmbitransitiveS + V / S + V + OThe door opened. / Rina opened the door.

4. Jebakan Umum

✗ They discussed about the novel.

✓ They discussed the novel.

✗ The children enjoyed.

✓ The children enjoyed the activity.

✗ The hero disappeared the village.

✓ The hero disappeared from the village.

Strategi TKA: setelah menemukan verb, tanyakan apakah ada unsur yang benar-benar menerima tindakan. Jangan menganggap semua kata setelah verb sebagai object.

5. Bacaan TKA

Learning Verb Patterns at a Community Library

A community library in Magelang launched a weekend program in which senior high school students helped younger children choose books and write short reviews. The volunteers had strong vocabulary, but the coordinator noticed a recurring grammar problem in their English summaries. Some students wrote sentences such as “The story happens” and thought every verb needed an object. Others wrote “The children enjoyed” without explaining what the children enjoyed. These mistakes revealed an important distinction: some verbs can complete their meaning without an object, while other verbs normally require one. During the first training session, the coordinator introduced intransitive and transitive verbs. An intransitive verb does not take a direct object. In “The children laughed,” the verb “laughed” is complete because the action does not pass to an object. A transitive verb, however, needs or normally allows an object that receives the action. In “The children borrowed three books,” the noun phrase “three books” functions as the direct object. The coordinator warned that the same verb can sometimes be transitive in one context and intransitive in another. “The meeting started at nine” uses “started” without an object, while “The librarian started the meeting at nine” uses it transitively. The volunteers tested verbs from actual book reviews. They found that “arrive,” “sleep,” and “disappear” are typically intransitive, so sentences such as “The hero disappeared suddenly” do not need objects. Verbs like “admire,” “need,” and “discuss” normally take objects: “Readers admire the hero,” “The family needs help,” and “The class discussed the ending.” They also learned to distinguish an object from a subject complement. In “The room became quiet,” “quiet” does not receive an action; it describes the subject after a linking verb. This distinction prevented students from calling every word after a verb an object. Next, the coordinator gave the volunteers a practical editing challenge. Each group received a paragraph in which several verbs had been used incorrectly. Students first asked whether the verb expressed an action that transferred to something or someone. Then they checked a reliable dictionary, because verb patterns cannot always be predicted from meaning alone. One group discovered that “explain” requires an object before a “to” phrase in sentences such as “She explained the rule to us.” Another group learned that “discuss” normally takes a direct object and does not need “about”: “They discussed the book,” not “They discussed about the book.” The training changed the quality of the reviews. Students began to choose verbs more carefully and to notice whether a sentence was structurally complete. They also realised that grammar affects style. Intransitive verbs can make descriptions feel immediate: “The door opened. Everyone froze.” Transitive verbs can identify responsibility more clearly: “The storm damaged the roof.” By the end of the month, the library displayed the best reviews on a bilingual noticeboard. The younger readers enjoyed the recommendations, while the volunteers gained a deeper understanding of sentence structure. They had learned that a simple sentence can contain rich information, but its verb pattern determines what grammatical elements must or may follow.
