1.7 The Complex Sentence: ‘Whose’; Defining/Non-defining Clauses

Possessive relative whose, identifikasi antecedent, essential vs supplementary information, comma, dan perubahan makna.

1. Penjelasan Lengkap

Whose adalah possessive relative determiner/pronoun yang menghubungkan antecedent dengan sesuatu yang dimiliki atau diasosiasikan: The woman whose photograph appears here is my grandmother. Bentuk ini dapat merujuk manusia, organisasi, dan dalam banyak konteks benda: a company whose products are exported worldwide.

ANTECEDENT + whose + noun + clause → The school whose library was renovated opened in 1958.

Defining relative clause diperlukan untuk mengidentifikasi referent: The students who interviewed elderly residents presented first. Tanpa clause, referent berubah atau menjadi terlalu luas. Non-defining relative clause hanya menambah informasi tentang referent yang sudah jelas: Rina, who interviewed her grandfather, brought an old radio. Clause ini dipisahkan comma dan tidak menggunakan that secara normal.

Comma dapat mengubah interpretasi: My brothers who live in Jakarta are visiting dapat menyiratkan hanya sebagian saudara tinggal di Jakarta. My brothers, who live in Jakarta, are visiting menyajikan lokasi sebagai informasi tambahan tentang seluruh kelompok yang dimaksud.

JenisFungsiContoh
WhosePossession/associationThe student whose project won is absent.
DefiningIdentifies nounThe objects that survived are displayed.
Non-definingAdds supplementary infoThe radio, which still works, is very old.

2. Jebakan

✗ Rina, that interviewed her grandfather, brought a radio.

✓ Rina, who interviewed her grandfather, brought a radio.

✗ The student who's project won...

✓ The student whose project won...

Whose ≠ who's. Who's adalah contraction dari who is/who has.

3. Bacaan TKA

Family History Exhibition

For a school family-history exhibition, students interviewed parents, grandparents, and neighbours about objects that had been kept for many years. Some brought old photographs, while others displayed letters, cooking tools, or pieces of clothing. The English captions quickly raised two related grammar questions. How should students show possession inside a relative clause, and when should a relative clause be treated as essential information rather than extra information? The teacher began with “whose.” Unlike “who,” which usually refers to a person as the subject of a relative clause, “whose” expresses possession or a relationship. “The woman whose photograph appears here is my grandmother” means that the photograph belongs to or is associated with the woman. Students were surprised to learn that “whose” can also refer to organisations and sometimes things: “The school whose library was renovated opened in 1958.” This pattern is often more natural than an awkward sequence with “of which,” although formal alternatives are possible. Next, the class examined defining and non-defining relative clauses. A defining clause is necessary to identify the noun. “The students who interviewed elderly residents presented first” means that only a particular group of students presented first. If the relative clause is removed, the reference changes. A non-defining clause gives additional information about a noun that is already clear: “Rina, who interviewed her grandfather, brought an old radio.” Because the name “Rina” already identifies the person, the clause merely adds extra information. Commas mark this non-defining status in writing and pauses often mark it in speech. The punctuation difference can change meaning. “My brothers who live in Jakarta are visiting” may suggest that only some of the speaker’s brothers live in Jakarta and that those brothers are visiting. “My brothers, who live in Jakarta, are visiting” suggests that all the brothers live in Jakarta and adds that fact as extra information. The students therefore learned that commas are not decorative. They signal how the writer expects readers to classify the information. The exhibition captions provided many opportunities for practice. One student wrote, “This is the sewing machine whose owner was my great-grandmother.” Another revised the sentence to make the relationship clearer: “This is the sewing machine that belonged to my great-grandmother.” The teacher explained that grammatical possibility does not always equal stylistic preference. Writers should choose the structure that communicates the intended relationship most naturally. During the exhibition, visitors enjoyed reading the English captions beside the family objects. The students noticed that relative clauses helped them fit background information into limited display space. They could identify exactly which person or object they meant, add non-essential details when appropriate, and express possession without repeating nouns. The project also gave them a more subtle lesson: grammar choices influence interpretation. A comma can tell readers whether information is essential; “whose” can connect a person or thing to something associated with it. By the end of the project, the students treated these features not as punctuation trivia but as tools for precise storytelling.
